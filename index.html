A judge called India's unemployed youth "cockroaches." Within a month, a satire account had more followers than the ruling party. Here's why I think that's the most honest political development India has seen in years.

The Insult That Became a Mirror

On 15 May 2026, the Chief Justice of India compared unemployed young Indians to cockroaches and parasites. He later clarified he meant people using fake degrees to enter respected professions. Doesn't matter. The internet doesn't do nuance, and honestly, neither does the lived experience of being twenty-something in this country right now.

Within a day, a political communications strategist named Abhijeet Dipke had registered a domain, written a manifesto, and launched something called the Cockroach Janta Party. Within a week it had more Instagram followers than the actual ruling party. Within a month, people were marching to Jantar Mantar in cockroach masks demanding a minister's resignation.

That escalation isn't really about an insult. It's about what happens when you finally hand a generation a word ugly enough to match how they already feel.

Read the Manifesto Before You Laugh

The name is satire — a direct jab at the Bharatiya Janata Party, spelled out in case anyone missed it. But the five-point manifesto underneath the joke is not a joke. No Rajya Sabha seat for a retiring Chief Justice. Criminal liability for anyone who tampers with a legitimate vote. Fifty percent reservation for women in Parliament and Cabinet, without inflating the size of either. Media licenses pulled from the conglomerates that happen to be close to the people currently in power.

Strip away the cockroach costumes and you're left with a fairly serious diagnosis of what's broken: a judiciary whose retired members keep landing in government appointments, an electoral system whose integrity keeps getting questioned, and a media landscape owned by two families who aren't exactly known for asking hard questions of the government.

Why a Meme Outgrew a Ruling Party

Within a week of launch, CJP had crossed roughly 19 to 20 million followers on Instagram. The BJP's own account sits at around 9 million. Congress, the main opposition, manages about 13 million. A movement with no office, no salaried staff, and no actual candidates was outperforming both of India's largest political organisations combined, using nothing but a joke and a manifesto written by one guy skipping sleep in Chicago.

You don't get numbers like that from people who think everything is fine. You get them from a country where youth unemployment has stayed stubbornly high for over a decade, where the most recent national medical entrance exam was hit by yet another leak scandal, and where an entire generation has watched "developed India by 2047" get promised to them every year while their own job market gets worse.

The Tell: They Blocked It

If you ever doubt whether a piece of satire has actually landed, watch what the target does next. Within days, CJP's official X handle became inaccessible inside India, with the platform citing a vague "legal demand." It came back within minutes under a new name: Cockroach is Back. A government secure in its standing wouldn't need to make a joke account disappear. You don't censor things that don't threaten you.

This is the same government that, by its critics' own long-running account, has spent over a decade tightening its grip on what gets broadcast, who gets to ask the Prime Minister a question on camera, and which journalists keep their jobs after asking the wrong one. A satirical party catching nearly twenty million followers in a week isn't a meme problem for that establishment. It's a legitimacy problem.

What I Actually Think Is Happening Here

I don't think the Cockroach Janta Party is going to contest the next election and win seats. I don't think Abhijeet Dipke is auditioning to be Prime Minister. I think what's happening is simpler and more important than that: an entire generation has finally found a register — funny, sharp, impossible to dismiss as "anti-national" — to say the thing it's been afraid to say directly for years.

When the country's most powerful judge calls you a parasite and the most effective response available to you is comedy, that tells you everything about how much room is left for the sincere version of that complaint. CJP isn't a punchline. It's a pressure valve for a decade of accumulated frustration that had nowhere else legal, safe, or loud enough to go.

Read the original reporting and the party's own manifesto, and form your own view.

Five weeks of protests, a selfie video from the PM, and a resignation nobody expected. Here's what actually happened — and why it matters more than the news cycle suggests.

The Sequence That Rewrote the Playbook

In late July 2026, something unusual happened in Indian politics. Five weeks after the Cockroach Janta Party began its protest demanding the resignation of the Education Minister over the NEET exam paper leak, Prime Minister Modi posted a rare selfie video from his phone saying he was pained by the students' suffering. Two days later, the minister stepped down. A youth-led movement — built on memes, Instagram reels, and cockroach masks — had just forced a cabinet resignation in a government not exactly known for backing down.

That hasn't happened in India at this scale before. Farmers protested for nearly a year before Modi repealed contentious farm laws in 2021. Students got a result in five weeks.

What Actually Drove the Protest

The trigger was a leaked medical entrance exam — NEET — which is the gateway to every government medical college in the country. When the leak was confirmed, it wasn't just a scandal. It was proof of something an entire generation had been quietly suspecting: that the system they were studying eighteen hours a day to pass was rigged at the gate itself.

But the underlying fuel was broader. Youth unemployment in India has stayed stubbornly high for over a decade. And now there's a new fear on top of it: that the IT back-office jobs India built its economic reputation on — the jobs that were supposed to be the safe destination for engineering and science graduates — are exactly the jobs AI is coming for first. A Reuters analysis published the same week noted that anxiety about AI replacing white-collar entry-level work was one of the strongest secondary drivers pushing young people into the streets.

Why This Particular Protest Cut Through

One detail keeps appearing in the analysis: a large number of people at the CJP protests were likely BJP supporters. That's a different beast entirely from opposition-led street movements, which the government can and usually does dismiss as politically motivated. You can't easily dismiss your own voters marching against you. A SOAS anthropology professor who studies Indian civil society put it plainly in Reuters reporting: this cross-over is what made the protest structurally different from anything in recent memory.

The opposition tried to capitalise, as oppositions always do. But the story wasn't about them. It was about a generation that had been handed a word ugly enough to describe how it felt — "cockroaches" — and decided to wear it like armour.

What Comes Next, Honestly

The next tests of this anger are state elections in Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, and Modi's own home state of Gujarat. Tamil Nadu already handed a template: an actor running on his own platform, propelled by an Instagram-first Gen Z fanbase, recently won the chief ministership. The 2029 national election is further out, but analysts who cover South Asia are now openly discussing what happens if CJP decides to contest seats.

I don't know if the movement survives long enough to matter at that scale. Movements have a way of getting absorbed, co-opted, or simply exhausted. What I do think is that this resignation proved something that a generation of activists had started to doubt: the system can still be moved. Not easily, and not by asking politely. But it can be moved.

When students marched in cockroach masks demanding accountability, most of India's biggest channels were busy with something else entirely. Here's what that tells you about whose side the press is actually on.

The Blackout That Wasn't Accidental

When the Cockroach Janta Party protests were drawing thousands to Jantar Mantar, a consistent pattern emerged: several of India's largest Hindi news channels — the ones with the most reach, the ones that shape what most of India thinks about most things — gave the protests minimal coverage or framed them as opposition mischief. Meanwhile, the BBC, Reuters, Al Jazeera, TIME magazine, and CNN International were all running full analytical pieces on what they were calling one of the most significant youth political movements India had seen in a generation.

The gap between how domestic and international press covered the same event wasn't subtle. It was the story inside the story.

What "Lapdog Media" Actually Means

The phrase appeared in international coverage — a BBC analysis headline explicitly used the term "lapdog media" in the context of Indian youth protests highlighting their distrust of mainstream outlets. That's not a fringe characterisation anymore. It's the phrase that young Indians themselves were using in their protest signs, and that foreign correspondents were now putting in headlines.

The structural explanation isn't complicated. Two large conglomerates own a significant portion of India's most-watched television news. Both have documented financial relationships — through advertising, through access, through a shared interest in regulatory goodwill — with the current government. That doesn't mean every editorial decision is a phone call from Delhi. It doesn't need to be. Institutional self-interest produces editorial caution without anyone needing to pick up a phone. The result is the same: stories that embarrass the government get shorter, later, softer. Stories that don't, don't.

Why Young Indians Already Knew This

The protests revealed something that pollsters and media critics had been documenting for years but that hadn't quite broken into public consciousness: a significant portion of Gen Z in India does not trust television news. They get their information from YouTube journalists, independent podcasts, international outlets accessible through VPN, and each other. The CJP itself was built entirely on Instagram and X. It didn't need a single minute of primetime television to cross 20 million followers.

That's new. Ten years ago, if the dominant TV channels didn't cover a protest, the protest didn't exist for most of the country. Now there's a generation that has built entirely parallel information infrastructure — and they're using it to organise against the very institutions that control the channels.

The Question I Keep Coming Back To

If India's mainstream press had covered the NEET leak and the student anger with the same urgency it gave to, say, celebrity divorce proceedings or IPL controversies, would the minister have resigned faster? Would the system have fixed the exam integrity problem sooner? Would fewer students have felt so cornered that they took their own lives after results they believed were manipulated?

I don't know. But I think it's worth asking. A free press doesn't just report on power — it applies pressure on it. When it stops doing the second part, the gap doesn't stay empty. Cockroach masks fill it instead.

Mandatory labels on deepfakes, three-hour takedowns, platforms losing safe harbour if they don't comply. The new IT Amendment Rules 2026 are India's boldest tech regulation yet. Here's why I'm genuinely split on them.

What the Rules Actually Say

Effective 20 February 2026, India's amended IT Rules introduced mandatory labelling for all AI-generated or AI-altered content. That means deepfake videos, cloned audio, manipulated images, and synthetic text all need clear labels — covering at least 10 percent of the visual surface area, or the first 10 percent of an audio clip's duration. Platforms must embed non-removable metadata and unique identifiers in files so origin can be traced across platforms.

The takedown timelines are aggressive: illegal AI content must be removed within three hours of a government order. Non-consensual deepfake imagery — the kind used to harass and destroy reputations — must come down within two hours of a complaint. Platforms that fail to comply lose safe harbour protection and face criminal prosecution. If a platform knowingly allows unlabelled deepfakes, it can be held directly liable for the content.

The Genuinely Good Part

Let's start where it deserves credit. The deepfake problem in India is real and it's getting worse fast. Fabricated celebrity videos circulated during the 2024 elections. Non-consensual intimate images generated using AI have destroyed careers and lives. The UNICEF has been sounding alarms about AI-driven tools being used to produce imagery targeting children. A government that waited longer to act on this would have been negligent.

The mandatory labelling requirement is particularly sensible. India has almost one billion internet users, spread across dozens of languages, varying levels of media literacy, and highly fractured information ecosystems. In that environment, the assumption that people can detect AI-generated content on their own is wrong. Labels help. Metadata trails help. Making platforms financially liable for knowingly ignoring this helps.

The Part That Should Make You Uncomfortable

The three-hour government takedown window is where the regulation gets complicated. The same government that moved its regulatory machinery so quickly against a YouTube comedy show — India's Got Latent — now has a legally backed mechanism to order the removal of AI-labelled content in less time than it takes to review whether the order is valid. Three hours is not enough time for a platform's legal team to assess whether a takedown request is legitimate. It's designed to be complied with first and challenged later, if at all.

Platforms that push back lose safe harbour. In practice, that creates an overwhelming incentive to over-comply — to take things down whenever the government asks, regardless of whether the content actually violates any provision. Add to that the vagueness in how "harmful" and "synthetic" content is defined, and you have rules that can be applied narrowly against genuine deepfake abuse, or broadly against political satire, opposition messaging, or journalism that uses AI tools to process data.

Where I Actually Land

I think the labelling provisions are genuinely good policy. The consent and victim-protection clauses are overdue. Holding platforms liable for knowingly hosting non-consensual imagery is correct. But the government takedown timeline, combined with safe harbour removal as the penalty, gives the state a regulatory lever that goes well beyond protecting victims from deepfakes. It creates a mechanism for fast content removal that doesn't distinguish between a fabricated harassment video and a satirical meme of a politician. Those are not the same thing, and the rules don't treat them differently enough.

Good regulation protects people from technology's worst uses without handing a government the same tools in the process. These rules do the first thing reasonably well. They don't do the second thing at all.

The government's stated purpose for the rules is protecting citizens from deepfake abuse and misinformation, not expanding content control. Read the rules themselves at meity.gov.in and form your own view.

One crude question on a YouTube show triggered FIRs, ministry advisories, and a national debate about censorship. The joke was bad. The response tells you a lot more about the country than the joke ever could.

The Joke, Briefly

In February 2025, podcaster Ranveer Allahbadia asked a contestant on the YouTube show India's Got Latent a crude hypothetical involving his parents. It was a bad joke. Not edgy, not clever — just bad, and it landed about as well as you'd expect. Within hours it was everywhere. Within days, host Samay Raina had pulled every episode of the show off YouTube, an archive built over almost a year, gone overnight.

I'm not here to defend the joke. I think it was genuinely unfunny, and Allahbadia has said as much himself. What I want to talk about is everything that happened after — because the response to one bad joke ended up looking less like child protection and a lot more like a regulatory machine that had been waiting for an excuse.

From One Bad Joke to a National Security Apparatus

Within ten days, the Ministry of Information and Broadcasting had issued a formal advisory to every OTT platform and self-regulatory body in the country, reiterating the Code of Ethics under the IT Rules. A parliamentary standing committee directed the IT Ministry to assess whether existing law needed tightening. FIRs were filed against Raina, Allahbadia, and other guests across multiple states. The Supreme Court itself said there was a "vacuum" in the law covering platforms like YouTube.

That is an extraordinary amount of state machinery to mobilise over one tasteless question on a comedy show. Compare that speed and scale to how slowly the same system tends to move on platforms that child-rights bodies had already flagged for genuinely obscene content, with far less urgency. The difference isn't the offensiveness of the content. It's who was watching, how viral the clip went, and how useful the outrage was to people who already wanted broader power to regulate online video.

The Pattern Is the Point

India's Got Latent didn't happen in a vacuum. The same year, a major Hindi news channel was made unavailable inside India on national security grounds. Tens of thousands of X accounts were blocked on government order, frequently without any cited post or evidence — among them Kashmiri news outlets, a national newspaper's deputy editor, and for a period, even Reuters' own handle. A respected English-language outlet was blocked for running a story sourced from international reporting, then unblocked only after public pressure. Independent analysis of these takedowns has pointed to one consistent thread that has nothing to do with obscenity or national security: criticism of the Prime Minister or of the ruling party's ideology.

Seen next to that pattern, the India's Got Latent advisory stops looking like an isolated reaction to a crude joke and starts looking like one more brick in a wall that was already being built — a wall that reaches for "obscenity" and "national security" interchangeably whenever convenient, and just happened to find an unusually useful pretext in a YouTube comedy show.

A Fair Note on the Disability Jokes

I want to be honest about one part of this story that isn't a free-speech issue at all: the show's pattern of jokes aimed at people with disabilities, which the Supreme Court later addressed by directing several of the comedians involved to issue public apologies and host disability-inclusive events. That's a different conversation from the obscenity panic, and a fair one. Punching down at a community that already gets a fraction of the accessibility and respect it deserves isn't edgy comedy, it's just cruelty with a microphone. Defending comedians from a censorship-hungry state doesn't require defending every joke they told.

Why I'm Still on Their Side

Samay Raina rebuilt the show, his comeback special told the story of how badly that year broke him, and India's Got Latent returned for a second season streaming on Netflix and YouTube in mid-2026 — proof the audience never actually left, even after the state did everything short of banning it outright. That's the part I keep coming back to. A government that genuinely cared about protecting minors from obscene content would apply a consistent, proportionate, content-neutral standard. What we got instead was a single viral clip turned into the justification for tightening control over online video that had clearly been drafted long before anyone heard that question about parents.

Bad jokes deserve criticism, sometimes a lot of it. They don't deserve to become the pretext for a state deciding it gets to define what an entire country is allowed to laugh at.

Engineering colleges are still grading us on things a model can now do in seconds. Here's why that's not a crisis — it's the clearest signal yet that the old plan was always a placeholder.

The Quiet Panic in Every Lecture Hall

Every semester, somewhere in a classroom full of engineering students, someone asks the question nobody wants to say out loud: if AI can already write the code, debug the circuit, or draft the report — what exactly am I being trained to do for the next forty years?

Professors don't have a clean answer. Neither do placement cells. The syllabus hasn't caught up, and it probably won't for a while. But the discomfort in the room is real, and I think it's worth sitting in rather than rushing past.

The Old Deal Is Breaking, Not the Industry

For decades, the deal was simple: study hard, get the degree, get placed, and the credential does the talking for the rest of your career. That deal worked when information and execution were scarce and expensive. Neither is scarce anymore. A model can already produce a passable first draft of code, a circuit simulation, or a report outline — the exact deliverables that used to define entry-level competence.

That doesn't mean engineers become irrelevant. It means the bar moves from "can you produce the artifact" to "can you judge whether the artifact is correct, and can you build the thing nobody's automated yet." Those are very different skills, and almost nobody is teaching the second one on purpose.

Why This Actually Helps the Honest Student

If your only skill was memorizing and reproducing, this is a genuinely uncomfortable moment. But if you were already the kind of person who builds things out of curiosity — who tinkers with a robotics project at 1 a.m. because the mechanism is interesting, not because it's graded — this shift quietly rewards you. Tools that compress execution time mean ideas move faster from sketch to working prototype. The constraint shifts from "can I make this" to "do I actually understand the problem worth solving."

That's a better game for a curious person to play than the one built around marks and rank lists.

The Real Risk Isn't Automation. It's Standing Still.

The danger isn't that AI takes engineering jobs. It's that an entire generation keeps optimizing for credentials built for a world that's already gone, while a smaller group quietly uses the same tools to compress years of learning into months and starts building things on the side before they even graduate.

I'd rather be in the second group. Not because I have it figured out — I clearly don't — but because the first principles question is finally honest: what can I build, understand, or fix that's actually useful, regardless of what's printed on the certificate?

That question was always the real syllabus. AI just stopped letting us pretend otherwise.

A deep dive into Skinner's Law and the pleasure-pain framework that governs human behavior. Learn how to hack your motivation when you find yourself avoiding important work.

Understanding Skinner's Law

In 1789, philosopher Jeremy Bentham mused on the two forces that control our lives: the pursuit of pleasure and the avoidance of pain. This general idea is the basis for the most useful productivity framework you've probably never heard of.

It's called Skinner's Law. The law is named after behavioral psychologist B.F. Skinner, who was a pioneer in the study of how rewards and punishments shape human behavior.

Skinner's Law says that to get yourself to do something, you need to either:

  • Make the pain of not doing it greater than the pain of doing it, or
  • Make the pleasure of doing it greater than the pleasure of not doing it

It's a very simple way of hacking your motivation when you find yourself procrastinating on or avoiding something important.

Practical Application

When I was writing my book, I kept saying it was my top priority, but weeks would pass without meaningful progress. Why? Ultimately, it was because of a pleasure-pain mismatch. Given how far out the deadline was, it was much easier (higher pleasure, lower pain) to skip writing on any given day than it was to sit down and work.

To fix this, I needed to adjust the equation. I made the pain of not writing greater by creating public accountability and setting up daily check-ins with a writing partner. I made the pleasure of writing greater by rewarding myself after each writing session.

The framework is simple, but the impact is profound. Next time you find yourself procrastinating, ask: How can I adjust the pleasure-pain equation?

Redefining Wealth

We live in a world that equates wealth with money. But true wealth is measured across multiple dimensions, and financial wealth is just one piece of the puzzle.

The Five Types of Wealth

1. Social Wealth: The strength of your relationships and community. This includes deep friendships, family bonds, and meaningful connections. No amount of money can replace the joy of genuine human connection.

2. Time Wealth: The freedom to spend your time as you choose. It's not about having unlimited time, but having control over how you use it. The wealthiest people guard their time fiercely.

3. Physical Wealth: Your health and vitality. No amount of money can compensate for poor health. Energy, strength, and wellness are the foundation for everything else in life.

4. Mental Wealth: Peace of mind, emotional well-being, and mental clarity. This is the foundation for everything else. Without mental wealth, no other form of wealth truly matters.

5. Financial Wealth: Yes, money matters. But it's a tool to build the other four types of wealth, not an end in itself. Money provides options, security, and freedom—but only when used intentionally.

Building True Wealth

The wealthiest people I know aren't necessarily the richest. They're the ones who have achieved balance across all five dimensions. Start tracking your wealth across all five types, and make intentional choices to build each one.

Break down complex problems to their fundamental truths. This is how Elon Musk, Aristotle, and other great thinkers approach challenges.

What Is First Principles Thinking?

First principles thinking is the practice of breaking down complex problems into their most basic, foundational elements—the "first principles"—and rebuilding from there.

Instead of reasoning by analogy (doing things because that's how they've always been done), first principles thinking asks: What are the fundamental truths? What do we know to be absolutely true?

The Elon Musk Example

When Elon Musk wanted to build SpaceX, everyone told him rockets were too expensive. The going rate to buy a rocket was $65 million. Instead of accepting this, he asked: What are rockets made of? What is the raw material cost?

He discovered that the materials cost was only about 2% of the typical rocket price. The rest was markup, inefficiency, and legacy costs. By rebuilding from first principles, SpaceX was able to reduce costs by 10x.

How to Apply It

1. Identify and challenge your assumptions
2. Break the problem down to fundamental truths
3. Create new solutions from the ground up

This approach works for business problems, personal challenges, and strategic decisions. The next time you face a complex problem, resist the urge to reason by analogy. Go back to first principles.

The Core of Stoicism

Stoicism, developed in ancient Greece and Rome, offers a practical philosophy for navigating life's challenges. At its core is a simple but profound insight: We cannot control what happens to us, but we can always control how we respond.

The Dichotomy of Control

The Stoics divided everything into two categories: things we can control and things we cannot. We can control our thoughts, actions, and responses. We cannot control other people, external events, or outcomes.

Most of our suffering comes from trying to control what we cannot control, and neglecting what we can. The path to resilience is accepting this dichotomy and focusing our energy accordingly.

Three Stoic Practices for Modern Life

Negative Visualization: Regularly imagine losing what you have. This builds gratitude and prepares you for potential loss. By mentally rehearsing loss, you become more resilient when challenges actually arise.

The View from Above: Zoom out and see your problems from a cosmic perspective. Most of what troubles us is small when viewed from this lens. This practice helps you maintain perspective and avoid being overwhelmed by minor setbacks.

Voluntary Discomfort: Deliberately practice discomfort (cold showers, fasting, hard workouts) to build resilience and reduce dependence on comfort. By choosing discomfort, you train yourself to handle involuntary discomfort better.

Building Unshakeable Strength

Stoicism isn't about suppressing emotions—it's about not being controlled by them. It's about building mental strength that serves you in both good times and bad.

Principles

What I Believe

Through exploration and self-discovery, I’ve shaped these core values:

1

Simple beats complex

The most powerful ideas are often the simplest. My goal is to take complex concepts and distill them into frameworks you can remember and apply. If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough.

2

Action beats theory

Knowledge without application is just trivia. Every framework I share comes with concrete steps you can take today. The gap between knowing and doing is where most people get stuck—I help you bridge that gap.

3

Timeless beats trendy

I'm more interested in principles that have worked for centuries than tactics that might work this quarter. Stoic philosophy, cognitive psychology, behavioral economics—these fields offer insights that remain relevant regardless of technological change.

4

Questions beat answers

The right question is more valuable than the right answer. I try to equip you with better questions to ask yourself, your team, and your work. Questions open doors; answers close them.
